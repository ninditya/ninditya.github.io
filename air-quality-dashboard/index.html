01 · Executive Summary

Three patterns the data actually supports.

Before the methodology: three statistically-tested patterns from the 2013–2017 dataset, with their scope and limits noted upfront.

Seasonal pattern (PM2.5)
Winter
Winter (Dec–Feb) has the highest share of high-PM2.5 hours of any season — binning PM2.5 into Low/Medium/High and testing against season gives χ² = 11,819 (p < 0.001), a statistically significant association. Composite AQI (which also weighs ozone) actually peaks in spring — see Technical Decisions for why.
Wind vs. pollution
Wind
Wind speed (WSPM) is negatively associated with AQI, most strongly in winter (Spearman r = −0.44). Pooled across the full period, other variables — pressure included — show comparable associations, so wind is one contributing factor, not a proven dominant one.
Spatial inequality
Urban
Urban stations report unhealthy-or-worse AQI readings more often than suburban or rural ones (58% vs. 47% of readings). Zone type is significantly associated with AQI category (χ² = 4,600, p < 0.001) — an association, not proof that location alone causes the difference.

Challenge

The Problem

  • Six raw pollutant concentrations per station, but no single standardized AQI value to compare severity across stations or time
  • No systematic test for whether the seasonal and spatial patterns visible in the raw data were real or just noise
  • Unclear which stations showed the most persistent poor air quality across the 2013–2017 period
  • Meteorological relationships (wind, rain, pressure) were assumed from domain knowledge but never checked against this dataset
Solution

The Approach

  • Computed an EPA-based AQI from 6 raw pollutant readings per station using EPA breakpoint interpolation
  • Ran Spearman correlation and chi-square tests to check whether visually apparent patterns were statistically significant
  • Built a heuristic composite score (PM2.5, AQI, gas index) to rank stations for exploratory comparison
  • Deployed as an interactive Streamlit dashboard with 5 analytical views
Impact

The Results

  • Season significantly associated with PM2.5 tier: χ² = 11,819, p < 0.001 (PM2.5 binned Low/Medium/High)
  • Zone type significantly associated with AQI category: χ² = 4,600, p < 0.001
  • Wind speed negatively associated with AQI, strongest in winter (r = −0.44)
  • Geospatial map ranks stations by composite score for exploratory comparison

AQI Calculation

The EPA uses piecewise linear interpolation to convert raw pollutant concentrations into sub-index values. The final AQI is the maximum across all pollutants:

AQI = max(AQI_PM2.5, AQI_PM10, AQI_O3, AQI_CO, AQI_NO2, AQI_SO2)

Six categories from Good (0–50) through Hazardous (301+). This standardization makes all 12 stations directly comparable despite raw measurement differences.

Priority Score Formula

Each station receives a heuristic composite score using Z-score normalized values — not a validated risk model:

priority = 0.5 × PM2.5_z + 0.35 × AQI_z + 0.15 × gas_index_z

This weighted score drives the geospatial map, sized and colored by priority tier. Because AQI is partly derived from pollutant concentrations, PM2.5 and AQI overlap rather than measuring independent things — and the 50/35/15 weighting is a heuristic choice, not a fitted or validated one. Treat the score as an exploratory ranking aid, not a risk model.

06 · Key Findings

What the data actually shows.

Seasonality
Winter = highest PM2.5
χ² = 11,819 · p < 0.001
Winter (Dec–Feb) has the highest share of high-PM2.5 hours of any season, plausibly linked to coal heating and temperature inversions — though this test shows association, not mechanism. This is a PM2.5-specific result: composite AQI, which also factors in ozone, actually peaks in spring (172.8 avg) rather than winter (157.3 avg).
Meteorology
Wind vs. AQI: weak, seasonal
Spearman ρ, WSPM vs AQI
Wind speed (WSPM) is negatively correlated with AQI, strongest in winter (r = −0.44). Across the full period it's a weak-to-moderate association, not the strongest one recorded — pressure showed a comparable negative correlation overall (r = −0.23 vs. WSPM's −0.13).
Spatial inequality
Urban > rural AQI
χ² = 4,600 · p < 0.001
Urban stations report unhealthy-or-worse AQI readings more often than suburban or rural stations (58% vs. 47% of observations). Zone type is significantly associated with AQI category — an association the chi-square test confirms, not a causal claim.
Rainfall
Rain: weak association with AQI
Spearman r ≈ −0.04 (very weak)
The dashboard's own correlation output describes the AQI–rainfall relationship as "very weak" (r ≈ −0.04). A wet-deposition washout effect is physically plausible, but this dataset doesn't provide strong quantitative support for it — a claim worth testing with better data (e.g. hourly pre/post-rain comparison) rather than asserting as confirmed.
Station ranking
Priority ranking: urban cluster tops the list
Heuristic composite score
The composite score (PM2.5 50% + AQI 35% + gas index 15%, all Z-score normalized) ranks the urban cluster highest. Since AQI is partly derived from PM2.5, the two aren't independent signals — best read as an exploratory ranking, not a validated risk score.
Critical period
Not winter mornings — June, 7pm
Heatmap peak: month 6, hour 19
The dashboard's own hour × month summary table flags June at 19:00 as the single highest-average-AQI combination (180.2) — not the winter-morning pattern intuition suggests. A plausible follow-up hypothesis is evening ozone formation compounding with traffic, but that wasn't tested directly here.
"The chi-square result — χ² = 11,819 for season vs. binned PM2.5 — shows the winter pattern isn't noise. It's also a reminder that composite AQI and PM2.5 don't always tell the same seasonal story."
07 · Technical Decisions

Every choice has a reason.

Four design decisions that shaped the validity of this analysis — including their limits.

Decision Alternative considered Why this choice
Spearman correlation Pearson correlation Meteorological variables here are skewed and their relationship with AQI isn't clearly linear. Spearman's rank-based approach doesn't assume linearity or normality — the trade-off is it only captures monotonic relationships, not the exact shape of the association.
Composite priority score Rank by PM2.5 only Ranking by PM2.5 alone ignores ozone and other gases captured in AQI. The weighted composite (PM2.5 50%, AQI 35%, gas index 15%) folds in more signal — but AQI is partly derived from PM2.5, so the two inputs overlap, and the 50/35/15 weights are a heuristic choice, not a fitted or validated one. Rankings should be read as exploratory.
IQR outlier detection Z-score cutoffs Z-score cutoffs assume a roughly normal distribution, which AQI readings aren't. IQR-based flagging doesn't make that assumption, so it's less likely to mislabel genuine extreme events (e.g. dust storms) as errors — though it still can't distinguish a real extreme from a sensor fault without manual review.
Chi-square for zone/season Visual inspection only A visual pattern could be sampling noise. Chi-square tests (p < 0.001 for both) turn "looks related" into a statistically testable claim — though the test establishes association, not that zone or season causes the difference.
08 · Learning Journey

This submission was rejected once — then passed.

This dashboard is the capstone for Belajar Fundamental Analisis Data — IDCamp 2025 Intermediate level on Dicoding. The first submission was rejected. Here's what changed.

❌ Submission #1 — Rejected
✗Business questions not Time-bound (SMART criteria)
✗EDA missing skewness, kurtosis, Q-Q plots, Shapiro-Wilk
✗No chi-square tests for categorical associations
✗Dashboard lacked dynamic time filters
✅ Submission #2 — Passed
✓All questions scoped to "2013–2017 period"
✓Full EDA: univariate + multivariate + normality tests
✓Chi-square tests added — χ² = 11,819 and χ² = 4,600
✓Interactive filters for station, date range, pollutant
Feedback #1 — Business Questions

Questions 2, 3, and 4 didn't meet the Time-bound aspect of SMART criteria. "When are the critical periods?" is conceptually valid but analytically unmeasurable. Adding "during the 2013–2017 period" transforms it from vague to testable.

Feedback #2 — EDA Depth

The univariate analysis was missing skewness, kurtosis, Q-Q plots, and Shapiro-Wilk normality tests. The multivariate section needed scatter plots with regression lines, pairplots, and violin plots. The categorical section lacked chi-square tests to validate visual patterns statistically.

Feedback #3 — Dashboard Interactivity

The Streamlit dashboard needed dynamic time filters and interactive controls so users can explore the data themselves — not just view static pre-computed output.
