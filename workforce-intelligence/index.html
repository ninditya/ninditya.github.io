The scenario

A healthcare distributor, five HR systems, and a payroll wall

The client is a nationwide healthcare distribution company with roughly 15,000 employees across 90 branches. Six months ago its CEO announced a digital-transformation mandate; the CHRO now needs to show AI-driven workforce decisions — who to reskill, which teams are at risk, which roles are heading toward obsolescence — without touching any of the underlying systems. She won't replace SAP SuccessFactors (HRIS), Workable (ATS), Moodle (LMS), or the branch-run performance tool, and she can't: they're what 90 branches already run on.

Those systems don't share an employee ID, don't agree on job titles, and — the complication that reshaped the whole plan — thirty days into the engagement, Legal blocked HR's own access to Oracle Payroll under the company's data-governance policy. Payroll was the one system with an unambiguous, complete roster. Losing it meant there was no longer an authoritative source of truth to resolve identity against, and the plan had to say so out loud rather than quietly assume one.

What I built to answer it

Rather than write the strategy in the abstract, I generated a seeded, deterministic 200-person dataset that reproduces this mess on purpose — missingness correlated with branch HRIS adoption rather than random, 70 raw job-title strings for 10 real roles, name variants, date conflicts, transfers mislabeled as terminations — then built the identity-resolution and confidence-scoring pipeline against it, and a prototype that makes the before/after difference something an HR manager can click through rather than take on faith.

Problem framing

Why skill-gap analysis, and not attrition prediction

"AI-driven workforce decisions" is a mandate, not a product. I scoped it down to one buildable thing by scoring three candidate PoCs against what the data could actually support in 90 days.

Candidate PoCData availabilityFeasibility in 90 daysVerdict
Attrition / productivity risk prediction Needs a clean historical outcome label. Ten transfers are recorded as termination-then-rehire under a new employee ID, and pre-2022 performance ratings sit outside the scale that's supposed to apply to them. Low. Training a classifier on a contaminated label is exactly the trap the brief's own "82% accuracy, three managers say it feels wrong" scenario describes — building it first means re-enacting that failure instead of explaining it. Deferred to Phase 2
Internal mobility / workforce planning Presupposes a resolved, canonical view of who does what, where — which is precisely what none of the five systems currently agree on. Low as a first PoC. Genuinely valuable, but it's a consumer of the identity-and-taxonomy layer, not a substitute for building it. Phase 2, after the data layer exists
Skill-gap analysis Usable today: the HRIS skill field (44.5% filled) plus LMS course completions, against a 10-role, 14-skill taxonomy I can hand-build from the observed title variants. High. Doesn't need a trustworthy historical label — only a defensible current-state read, which the confidence-provenance model can express honestly even where data is missing. Chosen for the 90-day PoC

It's also the smallest surface on which the actual argument — that the data layer changes the answer more than any model does — can be proven end to end in one quarter. That argument, not a specific algorithm, is the thing worth being right about first.

Explicitly not solved in 90 days

  • Attrition and productivity-risk prediction — blocked on the transfer/termination label contamination above.
  • Role-obsolescence forecasting over a 2-year horizon — needs longitudinal skill-demand trend data this engagement doesn't yet have.
  • Anything payroll-linked — cost-to-fill, comp benchmarking — Oracle is off the table for the duration.

What I'd spend the first two weeks learning

  • Real fill rate per branch and per system, pulled directly rather than assumed from the brief's headline "40%".
  • Which branches sit on which HRIS-adoption tier — the single variable that turned out to explain the entire naive-vs-resolved ranking gap.
  • Whether NIK or corporate email exists as a reliable join key between HRIS and ATS, since that governs whether high-confidence auto-merge is possible at all.
  • The exact scope of the Legal data-governance flag — payroll only, or anything payroll-adjacent mirrored elsewhere.
  • What "confidence" needs to mean to an HR manager in practice, from two or three short interviews, before the review-queue UI gets built rather than after.
Data strategy

Three layers, and nothing imputed silently

With Oracle gone, there's no authoritative roster left to arbitrate identity. I made HRIS the system of record by default — the only workable choice without payroll — and treated that as a named risk rather than a hidden one; it's the decision I'm least confident about, and I say why at the bottom of this page.

1

Ingestion

Raw pulls from HRIS, ATS, LMS and the performance tool, kept in native shape. Blank fields stay explicit nulls, never silently dropped — a payroll_id column is even kept in the schema and left empty, so what's missing is visible, not implied.

2

Identity resolution

A four-tier matching cascade (below) collapses 820 records into 232 resolved persons against a 200-person ground truth — a 16% over-split, which the resolution audit trail exists to make measurable instead of assumed.

3

Contextualization

Titles normalize against a 10-role taxonomy; skills get a four-status provenance and confidence score; anything under threshold routes to a review queue instead of a number.

Name normalization uses token-sort so "Wijaya, Ahmad Budi" and "Ahmad Budi Wijaya" collide. Branch is used to block the comparison space, but a date-of-birth match overrides that block — that's exactly the pattern a branch transfer produces, and it's the case blocking would otherwise hide. A single HRIS-only record is treated as the normal condition for anyone who joined before ATS adoption, not an anomaly — flagging it would flood the review queue with cases that don't need a human.

AI system design

A scoring engine you can audit, not a classifier you have to trust

For a first 90-day PoC where the data quality gap is the finding, I chose an auditable, rules-and-confidence scoring engine over an opaque classifier. Every gap score traces back to a per-skill status an HR manager can see, agree with, or contest — which is what a review queue is actually for.

StatusConfidenceSource
declared1.00Written directly in the HRIS skill field
evidenced0.90An LMS course completion maps to the skill
inferred0.55Required by the person's role, and only asserted if they have at least one other real signal
unknown0.00No signal at all — left visibly blank, never imputed

That "at least one other signal" clause on inferred is deliberate, not an edge case I missed: imputing skills for people with zero footprint anywhere would make chronically under-recorded branches look normal, burying the exact problem this PoC exists to surface. The system says "I don't know" instead of guessing with confidence.

The Day-75 question: 82% accuracy, but it feels wrong to three HR managers

Don't defend the metric — audit whether the model's top features correlate with something upstream of the outcome you're claiming to predict, rather than the outcome itself. In this dataset that variable is branch HRIS-adoption tier: the underlying skill reality is generated identically across every tier, yet a naive read produces a 0.461 spread in gap score across tiers, purely from record-keeping quality. The resolved, confidence-scored pipeline — same people, same skills — collapses that spread to 0.067, an 85% reduction.

Concretely: of the top 30 people a naive ranking would send to reskilling, only 10 also appear in the resolved ranking. The other 20 are people with zero recorded skills, not people with an actual gap — and 20 different people with a real, evidenced gap never surface at all. Three HR managers saying "these aren't the people I'd expect" are correctly detecting that mismatch before anyone can quantify it. The model learned branch record-keeping hygiene, not workforce risk, and 82% accuracy is the symptom, not the evidence.

Product & UX

Five screens, built so an HR manager never has to understand the model

The prototype is the argument made physically manipulable: an A / B toggle on every screen switches the entire interface between the naive union of three systems and the output of the contextualization layer, so the difference is something you click through rather than take on faith.

Decision Queue01

Teams ranked by gap, each row carrying a data-completeness badge and a one-click path to the person-level evidence behind it.

Needs Data02

Teams that can't be scored honestly yet, deliberately pulled out of the ranking — with what's missing and a prompt to complete the record, not send people to training.

Review Queue03

All 103 flagged records, filterable by five trigger reasons, so a data-ops person works the highest-impact category first, not chronologically.

Person Detail04

Every skill's status, confidence and source in one table, plus the full identity-resolution audit trail — tier, confidence, source systems, name variants observed.

Data Mirror05

A branch-by-branch HRIS fill-rate leaderboard — the Day-30 artifact, and the chart that turns "why is our score different" into something a CHRO can act on directly.

A / B toggle—

Present on every screen. On the naive dataset, Needs Data and Review Queue are disabled outright — a raw union of three systems has no confidence signal, so there's no honest basis for either.

Communicating uncertainty without wrecking trust

Uncertainty is a first-class value everywhere a number appears — a badge on every row, a confidence column on every skill, "cannot be determined" instead of a plausible-looking placeholder. The one choice that matters most: Needs Data is a peer navigation item, sized and positioned exactly like every other queue, not a footnote. That tells the HR manager the system is willing to say "I don't know" as confidently as it says "act here" — which is what makes the times it does give a number worth trusting.

Platform strategy

What survives past this one client

Reusable across the next five clients

  • The identity-resolution cascade as a config-driven service — tier rules and thresholds are parameters, not code.
  • The declared / evidenced / inferred / unknown provenance model, generalized to any workforce attribute, not just skills.
  • A configurable role/skill taxonomy — this client's 10 roles and 14 skills are one instance of a schema.
  • The Needs Data bucket and the review queue's trigger-reason filters — a pattern any future client's data-quality gaps can reuse.
  • The Data Mirror fill-rate view as a zero-model, Day-30-ready deliverable for any new client — useful before any AI is built.

What doesn't reuse

  • The specific taxonomy content — every client's roles and skills differ.
  • The specific messiness parameters — each client's systems will fail in their own way.
  • What travels is the shape of the pipeline, not its contents: ingest untouched, resolve identity with confidence, contextualize with provenance, never impute silently.

Least confident about

It's the only workable default once Oracle is gone — but it means every downstream number inherits HRIS's own blind spots, including the low-adoption branches this whole case study is about. I'd validate it in week one by checking how often HRIS and ATS disagree on active/terminated status for the same person; frequent disagreement means the default is quietly wrong in a way no confidence score would catch.
