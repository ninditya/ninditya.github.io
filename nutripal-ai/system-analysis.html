NutriPal — Product & System Analysis

An assessment of what NutriPal is today, who it is for, what the code already does, and what has to be true for it to become a product.

Prepared for the internal product, engineering and design team · Market focus: Indonesia

Try the interactive prototype → View technical diagrams →

1 · Executive summary

NutriPal is a working AI nutrition assistant built on Next.js 15, Supabase and Google Gemini 2.5 Flash. A member signs in, enters four biometric values, and from that point the product does three things: it tracks the day's energy balance, it turns a photograph of a meal into a nutritional record, and it recommends what to eat next — either from delivery apps the member already uses, from the ingredients in their kitchen, or as a full synthesised day plan.

The build is further along than a prototype. Five typed Genkit flows are in place, every AI response is schema-bound rather than free text, and the database is protected by row-level security with a dedicated RPC for concurrent macro increments. The four-key Gemini rotation is a pragmatic answer to free-tier rate limits and it works.

Three things stand between the current build and a product. The delivery catalogue is a four-item fixture rather than a live feed, so the strongest commercial mechanic is not real yet. Wearable data is mocked, which means the recovery-plan trigger cannot be trusted. And there is no retention loop at all — reminders are stored per meal but never delivered, and nothing brings a member back on day three.

Recommendation. Do not add features. Spend the next quarter making the three existing loops real: a live delivery catalogue, a real wearable connection, and a reminder that actually fires. Monetise on the Pro subscription plus delivery commission; defer fulfilment and B2B until the data-governance story exists.

2 · Problem statement and background

Indonesia's nutrition problem is measurable and getting worse. The 2023 Survei Kesehatan Indonesia put overweight and obesity among adults over 18 at 37.8 per cent, up from 35.4 per cent in 2018; obesity alone rose from 21.8 to 23.4 per cent over the same period, and central obesity — measured by waist circumference — climbed from 31 to 36.8 per cent. Roughly one adult in three is now above a healthy weight. Prevalence is highest exactly where smartphone use and food delivery are densest: DKI Jakarta leads the country on both obesity (31.8 per cent) and combined overweight-plus-obesity (48 per cent).

The behavioural driver is convenience. Cheap, fast, energy-dense food arrives in twenty minutes; the nutritional consequence of that order is invisible at the moment of ordering. Existing trackers do not help much here, for three reasons that are specific to this market:

  • Logging cost. Manual entry of an Indonesian dish — nasi padang, gado-gado, soto — is slow and inaccurate. International food databases do not carry them well.
  • No link to action. A tracker tells you what you ate. It does not tell you what to order instead, from the app that is already on your phone.
  • Generic advice. Very few tools take a hypertension marker or a shellfish allergy and change every downstream recommendation because of it.

The market is willing to pay for a better answer. Statista projects Indonesia's digital fitness and well-being apps segment to reach roughly US$1.6 billion by 2027, growing around 18 per cent a year, and regional analysts consistently name Indonesia among the strongest ASEAN opportunities for mobile-first health products. The category is growing; nobody has yet won the Indonesian food-logging problem specifically.

Problem statement

Urban Indonesians who order most of their food cannot see the nutritional consequence of a meal at the moment they choose it, and the effort of logging what they ate afterwards is high enough that they stop within a week.

5 · System and data model

The architecture is three tiers. The browser holds the Next.js App Router client and talks to Postgres directly through the Supabase client, with row-level security scoped to auth.uid() doing the isolation work. AI calls run as server actions, so the four Gemini keys never reach the client. Google AI and Supabase form the third tier. Five custom hooks — useUser, useProfile, useDailyLog, useDailyLogs, useMeals — subscribe to realtime channels, which is why a meal logged on a phone appears on a laptop without a refresh.

Four tables carry everything: users, user_profiles, daily_logs and meals. Two design decisions are worth keeping. Daily totals are only ever changed through the increment_daily_log RPC, so two devices logging at once cannot clobber each other's numbers. And every AI flow is bound to a Zod output schema, so a flow either returns a fully typed object or throws — the UI never parses free text and never renders a half-formed response.

8 · SWOT

Strengths

  • Photo-to-record works end to end and removes the main reason trackers get abandoned.
  • Every AI response is schema-bound, so the UI never has to defend against malformed output.
  • Health markers and allergies propagate into every recommendation, not just a warning banner.
  • Realtime sync and a concurrency-safe increment RPC — unusual rigour for an early build.
  • Recommendations end in an order, not a chart.

Weaknesses

  • Delivery catalogue is a four-item fixture; the commercial mechanic is not real.
  • Wearable data is mocked, so the recovery trigger is decorative.
  • No retention loop: reminders never fire, no streaks, no digest.
  • Anonymous identity cannot be recovered on another device.
  • English-only interface in an Indonesian-first market.
  • No accuracy benchmark, so quality claims cannot be defended.

Opportunities

  • A double-digit growth market with no dominant Indonesian food-logging product.
  • Rising diagnosed prevalence of obesity and hypertension creates a clinical referral path.
  • Delivery platforms want healthier-basket narratives; a curation partner is useful to them.
  • Corporate wellness and insurers buy adherence data once governance exists.
  • Indonesian dish accuracy is a defensible data moat that global apps will not build.

Threats

  • Model pricing or quota changes hit unit economics directly.
  • A delivery platform ships its own nutrition layer and closes the deep-link route.
  • PDP Law enforcement on health data raises the compliance floor.
  • MyFitnessPal, Yazio and Fita all have photo features and larger budgets.
  • A visibly wrong calorie number destroys trust faster than a missing feature.

9 · Business model and monetisation

Monetisation is undecided, so four options were assessed against a single question: can it be tested with the build that exists today? Two can.

Stream Mechanic Readiness Verdict
Pro subscriptionFree tier caps photo analyses per day; Pro unlocks unlimited scans, plan synthesis and full history.Needs a quota counter and a payment provider. The Pro badge already exists in the UI.Launch
Delivery commissionRevenue share on orders placed through curated recommendations.Needs a live catalogue and an attributable link; the deep-link route already exists.Launch
CookBuddy fulfilmentMargin on prepared meals cooked to a synthesised plan.Needs kitchen operations and delivery logistics. High margin, high complexity.Defer
B2B seatsCorporate wellness and insurers buying anonymised adherence reporting.Needs scale, a governance story and PDP-compliant aggregation.Defer

The pairing matters. Subscription revenue is predictable but caps the free experience, which is the acquisition engine; commission revenue grows with usage and costs the member nothing. Running both means the free tier can stay generous enough to build the habit while the highest-intent moment in the product — choosing what to order — carries the revenue.

One number decides whether this works: inference cost per active member per month against the Pro price. Until that is modelled, no price should be announced.

11 · Roadmap

Sequenced so that each phase makes the next one measurable. Nothing in phase one is a new feature; it is finishing what already exists.

Phase 1 — Make it real · Q3 2026 · 6–8 weeks

  • Real Google sign-in with anonymous-session upgrade (FR-14).
  • Live delivery catalogue replacing the fixture, with attributable order links (FR-07).
  • Scheduled meal reminders delivered as web push (FR-12).
  • Bahasa Indonesia localisation across every surface.
  • Consent copy, retention policy, data export and delete (FR-15).

Phase 2 — Make it accurate · Q4 2026 · 6 weeks

  • 200-dish Indonesian benchmark set with reference values; publish the error band.
  • Portion-correction step after analysis (FR-13); feed corrections back as evaluation data.
  • Real wearable connection through Health Connect and Apple Health (FR-11).
  • Response caching for repeat dishes to cut inference cost per member.

Phase 3 — Make it pay · Q1 2027 · 8 weeks

  • Free-tier quota counter and Pro subscription with local payment methods.
  • Commission agreement with at least one delivery platform.
  • Weekly digest and streaks; instrument day-7 and day-30 retention.
  • Unit-economics model reviewed monthly against real inference spend.

Later — conditional · 2027+

  • CookBuddy fulfilment, if delivery commission proves the demand.
  • B2B wellness and insurer reporting, once governance and scale exist.
  • Nutritionist review layer as a premium tier.

12 · Open questions

  1. What is the actual inference cost per active member per month? Every pricing decision waits on this.
  2. Is a formal partnership with GrabFood or GoFood achievable, or should curation be built on public catalogue data?
  3. Which single metric defines success in the next quarter — day-30 retention, meals logged per member per week, or paid conversion? Choose one.
  4. Does the product make a health claim? If it does, medical review and disclaimer language become mandatory rather than optional.
  5. Web-only, or a packaged mobile app? Push reminders and camera reliability both argue for the latter.
